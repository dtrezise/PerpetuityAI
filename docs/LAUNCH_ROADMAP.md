# Company launch roadmap

Status: execution plan  
Planning horizon: 12 months  
Last updated: 2026-10-07

## Launch definition

“Launch” means the company can responsibly sell and deliver a bounded paid pilot with a clear buyer, agreement, data boundary, operating process, evidence standard, and stop conditions. It does not mean presenting an unvalidated platform as production-ready.

## Phase 0 — Foundation, days 0–30

### Company

- Confirm name and trademark strategy with qualified counsel.
- Select and form the legal entity.
- Establish founder ownership, invention assignment, governance, and decision rights.
- Open company banking and accounting; define approval thresholds.
- Obtain insurance guidance appropriate to consulting, technology, cyber, and media risks.

### Customer discovery

- Build a target list of production companies, studios, agencies, business-affairs teams, and approved AI/VFX vendors.
- Conduct at least 12 structured problem interviews across buyer, user, risk owner, rights-holder, and vendor roles.
- Record evidence in the claim register; do not count generic interest as demand.

### Pilot readiness

- Finalize the transaction definition and pilot packet.
- Engage qualified legal counsel for pilot terms and matter-specific boundaries.
- Approve the data inventory, tools, access matrix, retention, incident path, and security minimums.

### Exit gate

Proceed when one accountable buyer has a real in-scope transaction, budget, stakeholder access, and willingness to sign a paid pilot agreement.

## Phase 1 — First paid pilot, days 31–120

- Execute the pilot playbook.
- Keep raw biometric and performance assets with approved custodians.
- Measure baseline and outcome.
- Maintain weekly risk, exception, and decision logs.
- Produce a redacted case study only with written permission.
- Make an explicit build, revise, or stop decision.

### Exit gate

Proceed to productization only if the sponsor pays, wants repeat use, measurable friction declines, the packet survives review, and delivery can become less founder-dependent.

## Phase 2 — Repeatability, months 4–8

- Run two to four additional paid transactions in the same narrow workflow.
- Standardize the schema, role model, evidence export, and integrations requested repeatedly.
- Build only the repeated manual steps with clear buyer value.
- Establish secure development, identity, logging, deletion, backup, incident, and vendor-management practices.
- Develop bottom-up pricing using delivery cost, support load, buyer budget, and observed value.

### Exit gate

Proceed to enterprise software when buyers repeat, implementation time falls, gross-margin potential is credible, security requirements are achievable, and a narrow integration strategy is clear.

## Phase 3 — Product and assurance, months 9–12

- Implement the minimum multi-tenant control plane.
- Complete independent security testing.
- Formalize privacy, security, and AI-governance policies.
- Select an appropriate assurance roadmap based on buyer requirements.
- Publish only measured claims and permissioned case evidence.
- Evaluate fundraising against a validated capital plan rather than legacy estimates.

## Metrics

### Evidence

- Percentage of external claims with current support
- Number of unresolved high-impact claims
- Source freshness and citation-fit error rate

### Customer

- Paid pilot conversion
- Time from first meeting to signed pilot
- Repeat transaction and renewal intent
- Referenceability with written permission

### Workflow

- Request-to-decision time
- Clarification loops
- Missing or conflicting fields
- Rework after handoff
- Closeout evidence completeness

### Delivery

- Founder hours per transaction
- Support cost
- Implementation time
- Gross-margin path
- Security and privacy exceptions

## Stop conditions

- No accountable buyer will pay for the bounded workflow.
- Buyers prefer incumbents already in their stack.
- Value depends on holding sensitive assets the company cannot protect or justify.
- Authority cannot be represented reliably enough for reviewer use.
- Each transaction remains bespoke with no declining delivery cost.
- Legal, labor, contractual, or partner constraints make the wedge nonviable.
